Method

Every gameweek was decided with data that existed before that deadline: statistics through GW n−1, prices as at GW n, the prior season through a cross-season identifier, and news from dated pre-deadline sources only. No completed gameweek was ever re-run, and model improvements applied forward only — the GW1 squad was picked by the GW1 model, not by the one that finished the season.

The comparison line is the official average manager score for each gameweek, not a synthetic template. Autosubs, sell prices, free-transfer banking and the hit charge are simulated with real FPL mechanics. The replay is reproducible from the committed per-gameweek player files.

Where the points came from

Four behaviours produced the edge. None of them is a projection.

  • Refusals. Six separate proposals to churn a fully fit Saliba were vetoed; the eleventh Saliba-to-Timber variant was refused at GW17. A −4 that clears the expected value gate is still wrong if it breaks the standing multi-week plan. Net cost of the vetoes across the season: approximately zero, and each was right at the time it was made.
  • Holding through absence. A one-week knock is a bench call; a multi-week injury is a sale. Rice was benched on a precautionary knee at GW19 and returned 17 the next week. Enzo was held through Manchester City away and returned 11. Palmer was held through the mass sell-off. Selling and re-buying pays the spread twice and admits the first move was wrong.
  • Buying runs, one week early. Haaland was entered at GW6, ahead of the 865,000-manager rush, for 16 points and the armband. Mateta's entry was deliberately delayed a week to avoid his worst fixture: the deferral cost nothing and the entry gained seven.
  • Fading the template on the numbers. At GW8 the most-bought player in the game was faded for the one the crowd was selling — a seven-point horizon gap decided it. Outcome: 8 against 7, and £0.9m cheaper. At GW24 the same test picked Fernandes over Mbeumo on horizon and value; right by eight points.

The one costly discipline failure is also recorded: at GW19 the positional-duel read named a clean-sheet bet the optimizer benched on a 0.06-point margin. He returned seven from the bench. The override existed and was not pulled. The rule now says every sub-0.2 bench margin is listed in the memo with an explicit manager call before the deadline.

Calibration: what the projections actually did

Over the first twenty gameweeks the projections summed to 1076.2 against 1179 actual — a mean under-prediction of +5.14 per week. That gap was attributed to the doubled captain for most of the season. A full decomposition disproved it.

ComponentMean errorStdReading
Base XI, captain counted once+4.2612.63Carries all of the level bias — and it is within noise (t = 1.47, not significant)
Captain slot, the extra copy−0.074.98Mean-unbiased. High variance, not high bias: the leverage is real, the error is not
Autosubs+1.15—+23 all season, 21 of it in two weeks that covered surprise blanks
Hits−0.20—−4 all season, GW2

The conclusion was to change nothing in the model. A uniform additive correction is provably a no-op on every pick and on the hit gate — it adds the same constant to every feasible squad and cancels in the marginal comparison. A multiplicative one would scale the gain but not the fixed −4, flipping borderline hits on noise. The ranking was validated in all twenty weeks. So the fix went into reporting: every memo now states its predicted total as base XI plus captain slot, and the expected-value margin is read against a variance that is now measured rather than assumed.

One per-player signal survives as a watch item rather than a patch: starting midfielders are under-predicted by roughly 1.2 points each, traced to a stale bonus proxy. A per-position bump would reorder midfielders against forwards, so it waits for the minutes rebuild instead of being applied blind.

Chips: the counterfactual

No chip was played, which was itself a decision. The eight-gameweek Africa Cup of Nations window is the textbook wildcard trigger; it was replayed both ways.

Wildcard held at GW17236Against 238 on free transfers alone. The chip froze a squad that then needed two forced injury sales — the window's value was reactivity, and free transfers supplied it
Free Hit+6 onceNegative in every other week tested
Bench Boost+11 / +12 / +19 / +5Positive every week after the fact, but driven by bench variance nobody could forecast — a known-absent player sat on one of those benches. Only a real call when all fifteen are nailed starters, meaning a double gameweek
Triple Captain+16The cleanest chip: a premium captain on a standout single fixture

The rule that came out of it, now encoded in the chip-timing module and surfaced at every deadline: triple captain on a standout fixture or a double gameweek; bench boost only on a double; wildcard or free hit only for a genuine four-plus change need or a blank, never for a disruption that free transfers can ride.

What this does not prove

The honest limits matter more than the headline, because the 2026/27 season will test them.

  • The rank is an estimate. Tier-level cumulative totals for 2025/26 were not retrievable in this environment. Reconstruction puts the top-1% threshold near 570–600 at GW10 against our 624, which is top-10k pace — a trajectory, not a verified rank. Claiming the rank outright would overstate the evidence.
  • A replay is not a season. Team news was reconstructed from dated pre-deadline sources, and the historical data carries no injury flags — availability was modelled with a blank-run proxy plus researched overlays. Careful reconstruction still runs kinder than a live Friday press conference.
  • Twenty-four weeks of one season. No double gameweek, no blank, no chip actually played, and a captain who happened to be the correct ~90%-owned shield throughout. Fourteen gameweeks and the entire chip layer are untested.
  • The level bias is unresolved, deliberately. +4.26 per week in the base eleven is within noise over twenty weeks. It was closed as a reporting fix rather than modelled away, which is the right call on this sample and a re-open candidate on a longer one.

What carries into 2026/27

Three things are worth transplanting, and one is worth building.

  • The refusal discipline. Every hit checked against a standing multi-week plan, not only against the expected-value gate. This produced more edge than any modelling improvement in the run.
  • The written overlay. Locks, bans, captain and lineup overrides are mechanical inputs with a reason attached, which makes them gradeable in review. Silent deviation is what stops a system from learning.
  • The weekly review as a defect channel. Seven fixes came out of it mid-run. A season is thirty-eight chances to improve the model that picks the next squad.
  • Still to build. Multi-period transfer paths native to the optimizer — the hand-written plan produced the AFCON window's edge and deserves to be model-derived — plus a live calibration loop reading the committed projection files each week.

The 2026/27 season is the live test, and the first after a World Cup summer: late returners, early rotation, and minutes uncertainty everywhere. That is precisely the market the minutes model was built to price.
